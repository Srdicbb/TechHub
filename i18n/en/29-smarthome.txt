KNX is an open standard for home and building automation. Devices sit on a shared two-wire **bus**, each has its own processor and parameters, and commands travel as **telegrams** addressed to **group addresses**. A button doesn't know which relay it switches: it sends to a group address, and every device listening to it reacts.
@b8d35ab7d6
The **bus** (TP) is a two-wire cable that carries both power and data. The line power supply provides about 30 V DC that the devices share, so the number of devices and consumption per line are planned in advance.
@4d7c36cdbb
The **physical address** (e.g. `1.1.12`) says where a device is: area, line, device. It is used for programming and diagnostics.
@f2bc376aaa
A **group address** (e.g. `1/0/3`) is a function, for example “living room light, switch on”. The button sends to it, and every actuator listening to it reacts; one device can listen to several group addresses.
@8362e78137
The same function usually has two addresses: **command** and **status**. The actuator reports the real state on the status address, and panels and visualisation read that one.
@c0a884ab91
Everything is configured in **ETS**: the project, addresses and device parameters. The devices are then programmed over the bus or an IP interface.
@31777746f2
A **KNX IP** interface or router connects the bus to the network: for ETS, for visualisation and for integration with Home Assistant or other systems.

Calculation · current and voltage drop on an LED strip
@75a81e2412

Strip:   5 m, 14.4 W/m, fed from one end
Power:   5 m × 14.4 W/m = 72 W
Current: at 12 V → 72 W / 12 V = 6 A        at 24 V → 72 W / 24 V = 3 A

Voltage drop grows with current (I · R), and power loss with the square of the current (I² · R).
At 24 V the current is halved, so the voltage drop is halved too,
and the loss in the strip's tracks is four times smaller.
Feed a long strip from both ends or from the middle, and choose the cable
from the power supply to the strip by current and length.

**Matter** is a common application standard for smart-home devices: the same device works with several ecosystems and is controlled locally, over the IP network. **Thread** is a low-power wireless IP mesh network that Matter devices often use, and it joins the home network through a **border router**.
@bcd180fd89
Matter runs over IP: Wi‑Fi, Ethernet or Thread. Commands stay in the home network, so internet is not needed for control.
@9d422ac52e
**Thread** is a mesh like Zigbee, but every device has its own IPv6 address. Mains-powered devices are routers, and battery ones are end devices that mostly sleep.
@2d4a1ee805
A **border router** connects the Thread mesh to the home network. There can be several; when one disappears, traffic goes through another.
@c6fda79ff9
**Commissioning**: a device is added by scanning a QR code or entering a pairing code; it then gets a certificate and joins the controller's **fabric**.
@f7337dd8a8
**Multi-admin**: the same device can be in several fabrics at once, so two ecosystems control it, for example the manufacturer's app and Home Assistant.
@16170cb4a9
Matter describes devices through **clusters** (On/Off, Level Control, Color Control). Whatever the standard doesn't cover stays in the manufacturer's app, so support is not the same everywhere.
@1f394b5ac1
Matter · adding a device (commissioning)
@57bd1358d4

1. The device advertises over BLE (or on the network) and waits for a pairing code.
2. The controller scans the QR code and gets the code and the device details.
3. The device certificate is checked: that it is a genuine, certified product.
4. The controller gives the device its network credentials: the Wi-Fi password or the Thread network details.
5. The device gets an operational certificate and joins the controller's fabric.
6. For a second ecosystem: open a pairing window in the first one and repeat the procedure.
@18faf315d3
A Thread device in a house with no border router has no way to reach the home network. A border router is usually built into a hub, a smart speaker or a router.
@446219ce7a
Several separate Thread networks from different manufacturers: devices are split, and each mesh is weaker. It helps when border routers share the same Thread network.
@9076be6631

**DALI** is a two-wire digital bus for lighting. Every driver gets a short address from 0 to 63, can belong to up to 16 groups and stores up to 16 scenes. The controller sends a command to one address, a group or everyone at once, and drivers can also answer: state, level, lamp failure.
@a617390a40
The bus has its own power supply (usually about 16 V DC) and runs slowly, at 1,200 bit/s, which is enough for lighting: a command takes a few tens of milliseconds.
@64fea82dad
DALI is not safety extra-low voltage (SELV): the bus wires may run in the same cable as the luminaire supply, but they are treated as mains voltage. Polarity doesn't matter for most devices.
@041d86afbd
A **short address** is for one luminaire, a **group** for several (for example everything in an office), and **broadcast** for everyone on the line.
@ccafaa1929
A **scene** is stored in the driver itself: the controller says “scene 2”, and each driver knows its own level for that scene.
@e5e49a83fa
**DALI-2** also standardises input devices (buttons, sensors) and compatibility testing; **DT8** is the device type for light colour (tunable white, RGBW).
@a8369a8acf
One line takes up to 64 drivers; for more, lines are added, and the system connects to KNX or a smart-home controller through a gateway.
